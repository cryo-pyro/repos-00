Latency ($UB$) breaks this causality. If there is a delay between feeling the terrain and moving your foot, you are effectively navigating a map that no longer exists.

1. The Mechanics of "Stale" Steps

When you have latency ($\tau$), your update rule changes from reacting to the present to reacting to the past.

  • Standard SGD (Instant): $x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta \cdot \nabla y(x_t)$
    • Interpretation: "I am here ($x_t$), the slope is steep, I step down."
  • Delayed SGD (Blind): $x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta \cdot \nabla y(x_{t-\tau})$
    • Interpretation: "I was there ($x_{t-\tau}$) 5 seconds ago, the slope was steep then, so I will take a big step now."

The Result: The Oscillating Crash

Because you are applying an old correction to a new position, you inevitably overshoot.

  • You step left to correct a rightward tilt.
  • By the time the step lands, you are already leaning left.
  • The "old" instruction pushes you further left.
  • You spiral out of control.

In the visual above, notice how "blind" steps (without momentum or with poor tuning) result in jagged, inefficient zig-zags. Latency magnifies those spikes, turning a zig-zag into a divergence.

Optimization & Engineering

"Momentum per dollar" is a neat way of asking:

How much physical "push" (momentum) do I get for each unit of money I spend?

It shows up in physics, engineering, defense, transport, and even economics when people compare efficiency of force vs cost.

Let's unpack it.

1. In Physics Terms

Momentum is:

$$p = m v$$

where

  • $m$ = mass
  • $v$ = velocity

If something costs $C$ dollars, then:

$$\textbf{Momentum per dollar} = \frac{p}{C} = \frac{mv}{C}$$

So:

How much mass × speed am I buying with my money?

2. Intuition

Think of it like:

  • 🚗 Buying vehicles
  • 🚀 Launching rockets
  • 💣 Designing weapons
  • ⚙️ Building machines

You're asking:

"For every $1 I spend, how much moving power do I get?"

Higher = more "bang for buck" (literally sometimes).

3. Examples

Example 1: Two Vehicles

Vehicle Mass Speed Cost Momentum p / $
A 1000 kg 20 m/s $10,000 20,000 2
B 500 kg 40 m/s $20,000 20,000 1

Both have same momentum, but:

  • A gives double momentum per dollar

So A is more "efficient".

Example 2: Projectiles / Defense

In ballistics:

$$\frac{mv}{\text{cost}}$$

tells you:

Which projectile delivers more impact for less money.

That's why militaries care about this ratio.

4. Energy vs Momentum per Dollar

Important distinction:

Metric Formula Measures
Momentum $mv$ Push / impulse
Energy $\frac{1}{2} mv^2$ Damage / destruction

So you can also define:

  • Energy per dollar
  • Momentum per dollar

They optimize different things.

Example:

  • Fast light object → high energy
  • Slow heavy object → high momentum

5. Engineering Meaning

In engineering/transport:

High momentum per dollar means:

  • ✅ Cheap materials
  • ✅ Efficient propulsion
  • ✅ Good mass utilization
  • ✅ Low waste

Example: Freight shipping

Ships > Trucks > Planes

Why?

Ships have highest momentum per dollar.

They move massive weight cheaply.

6. Economic / Metaphorical Use

People also use this metaphorically:

"Which investment gives me the most momentum per dollar?"

Meaning:

  • Most growth
  • Most influence
  • Most leverage

Same structure:

$$\frac{\text{Impact}}{\text{Cost}}$$

7. General Formula

If you want a universal version:

$$\boxed{ \text{Momentum per dollar} = \frac{mv}{C} }$$

Where $C$ includes:

  • Purchase
  • Fuel
  • Maintenance
  • Labor
  • Risk

(Real systems always include all costs.)

8. Deep Insight

Maximizing momentum per dollar usually means:

Favor mass over speed, if cost scales with speed.

Because:

  • Speed is expensive (fuel ∝ $v^2$ or $v^3$)
  • Mass is cheap (steel, water, sand)

That's why:

  • Ships > Planes
  • Trains > Trucks
  • Slings > Bullets (historically)
  • Logs > Missiles (in some contexts)
