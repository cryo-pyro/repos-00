Classic SGD metaphors treat the loss landscape as continuous — the gradient exists everywhere, optimization is smooth descent. But biological systems are not smooth. They have gates.

A gate is a threshold event: below it, the gradient doesn't exist. Above it, the gradient is suddenly visible — and everything downstream changes. This is why "normal labs" can be profoundly misleading. The system is operating in pre-gate territory, maintaining homeostasis at metabolic cost. The cry, the symptom, the breakdown — these are the gate firing.

Phase II dynamics (threshold) and Phase III dynamics (gradient fate) are clinically distinct. Mixing them up produces the wrong interventions. These two cases illuminate the difference.

Synthesis

The Gate and the Gradient Are Not the Same Thing

These two cases reveal a distinction that the SGD framework makes visible and classical diagnosis obscures:

Phase II (Gate): The loss landscape has a threshold. Below it, no gradient exists — no optimization is possible, and attempting to descend in that region is pure noise (or hidden L_s foraging). The colonoscopy was injected into a zero-gradient region. The gate had not fired. The signal never crossed the threshold. There was nothing to optimize.

Phase III (Gradient Fate): A gradient exists — it is real, large, and loading. But homeostasis can hide it. "Normal labs" are not evidence of no gradient. They are evidence of a system spending reserve to maintain the appearance of no gradient. The cry was the gate. Once the gate fires, the gradient becomes visible. Now the question is: will it be steepened, flattened, cancelled, or prevented?
